The UK research community is facing a period of significant uncertainty following funding reductions and savings measures announced by the Science and Technology Facilities Council (STFC). While public attention has focused on concerns over high-profile facilities such as Jodrell Bank Observatory, the wider implications extend far beyond a single institution. The decisions affect a broad portfolio of national research infrastructure, including facilities such as the Diamond Light Source, ISIS Neutron and Muon Source, and the Central Laser Facility, all of which support thousands of researchers from universities, national laboratories, industry, and overseas partners.

Importantly, these are not simply "cuts to physics". Although STFC is often associated with particle physics, astronomy, and nuclear physics (PPAN), a substantial proportion of its activities support multidisciplinary research infrastructure used by scientists across many disciplines. Researchers in materials science, chemistry, clean energy, quantum technologies, life sciences, environmental science, archaeology, cultural heritage, and medicine depend on access to these facilities. The consequences therefore reach well beyond the physics community, affecting research that underpins healthcare, energy security, advanced manufacturing, and emerging technologies, all areas laid out in the Modern Industrial Strategy.

What is at stake is not only individual research projects but also decades of accumulated expertise and national capability. Large-scale scientific facilities rely on highly skilled scientists, engineers, technicians, software developers, and specialist support staff whose knowledge cannot be replaced quickly once lost. Reduced operating time, delayed upgrades, and uncertainty over future investment risk weakening the skills pipeline that supports UK science, from students considering STEM careers to early-career researchers building long-term careers in research and innovation.

Many members of the research community therefore view the current funding reductions as a strategic issue for the UK's future scientific and economic competitiveness. They argue that sustained investment in national research infrastructure is essential to maintaining international leadership, supporting the ambitions of the UK's Industrial Strategy, attracting global talent and partnerships, and ensuring that future generations inherit the skills, facilities, and expertise needed to address major societal challenges. The debate is therefore not only about budgets, but about the long-term future of the UK's research ecosystem and its capacity to deliver scientific discovery, innovation, and economic growth.
